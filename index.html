How to Use a Pad

Watch a step-by-step demonstration

Quick Tips:
  • Change your pad every 4–6 hours
  • Wash hands before and after
  • Wrap and dispose of used pads properly
  • Keep a spare in your bag

Step-by-Step Instructions

Follow these simple steps

  1. Wash your hands thoroughly with soap and water.
  2. Remove the pad from its wrapper and unfold it completely.
  3. Peel off the backing strip to expose the adhesive.
  4. Center the pad in your underwear with the adhesive side down.
  5. Press down firmly to help it stick.
  6. If the pad has wings, wrap them around the underwear.

It's normal to feel nervous the first few times. With practice, it's quick and easy.

Menstrual Product Comparison

Explore options to find what works best for you

Disposable Pads

Absorbent pads that stick to underwear.

  • Pros: Easy for beginners, widely available, good for heavy flow
  • Cons: Can feel bulky, creates waste, can cause odor if not changed

Tampons

Absorbent cylinders inserted into the vagina.

  • Pros: Great for sports/swimming, discreet
  • Cons: Must change within 8 hours, insertion learning curve

Menstrual Cups

Reusable silicone cups that collect flow.

  • Pros: Eco-friendly, up to 12 hours wear
  • Cons: Cleaning/sterilizing, higher learning curve

Period Underwear

Underwear with built-in absorbent layers.

  • Pros: Reusable, comfortable
  • Cons: Higher upfront cost, may not be enough on heavy days

Menstrual Hygiene Best Practices

Keep healthy and comfortable during your period

Daily Care

  • Change pad or tampon every 4–6 hours
  • Wash hands before and after
  • Use warm water + mild soap for cleaning (external only)
  • Always wipe front to back

Proper Disposal

  • Wrap used pads in toilet paper or wrapper
  • Never flush pads or tampons
  • Use bins when available

Clothing & Comfort

  • Wear breathable cotton underwear
  • Keep extra underwear/products with you

What to Avoid

  • No scented products on the vulva
  • Avoid douching
  • Don’t leave tampons in > 8 hours

Why Menstrual Hygiene Matters

Health benefits and warning signs

Health Benefits

  • Prevents infections
  • Reduces risk of UTIs
  • Prevents toxic shock syndrome (TSS)

Comfort & Confidence

  • Prevents irritation and rashes
  • Helps you stay active and social
Warning signs to watch for: severe pain, very heavy bleeding, strong/fishy odors, fever, or unusual discharge—see a healthcare provider.

Understanding Your Reproductive System

Anatomy + the menstrual cycle

Female Reproductive System Diagram

Organ Functions

  • Uterus: The lining (endometrium) thickens each month and is shed during menstruation if pregnancy doesn't occur.
  • Ovaries: Store eggs and produce hormones (estrogen, progesterone). Release one egg each cycle.
  • Fallopian Tubes: Connect ovaries to the uterus; the egg travels through here after ovulation.
  • Cervix: Lower part of the uterus that connects to the vagina; cervical mucus changes across the cycle.
  • Vagina: Muscular canal where menstrual fluid exits.

The Menstrual Cycle

Typical cycle length is ~28 days (can range 21–35 days). Phases:

  • Days 1–5: Menstruation (shedding of the uterine lining).
  • Days 1–13: Follicular phase (egg development).
  • Day 14: Ovulation (egg released from the ovary).
  • Days 15–28: Luteal phase (lining maintained; if no pregnancy, cycle restarts).

Key Hormones

  • Estrogen: Builds the uterine lining and supports the first half of the cycle.
  • Progesterone: Stabilizes the lining after ovulation.
  • FSH: Stimulates egg development in the ovary.
  • LH: Triggers ovulation.

Ask a Doctor – Q&A with Dr. Vani Venkatachalam

Questions from young girls about menstruation

1. Why are my periods so different from my friends?
Every girl's body is different. Some have shorter periods than others; some bleed less and some bleed more. Go to the doctor if your period is excessively painful, if it lasts longer than seven days or makes you skip school. These are signs of a hormonal imbalance or excessive menstrual bleeding.
2. Is it bad if I skip a month sometimes?
Period skipping is normal, especially for the first few years. Your body is still adjusting to it all. Still, if you miss for several months or experience accompanying symptoms like a weight change or hirsutism, it wouldn't hurt to see a physician.
3. Why does my mood get really bad before my period?
This is probably premenstrual syndrome or PMS. Right before your period, your hormones drop and this may cause sadness, anger, anxiety. You can try journaling, talking to someone, and light exercise such as walking or stretching. If you're concerned, mention it to a physician; it could be PMDD which is a more extreme situation, but something that can be treated.
4. I feel super tired during my period. Is that normal?
Yes, that is normal. You're losing blood and your body's working overtime. Make sure you eat iron-rich foods (beans, leafy greens, fortified cereals) and hydrate. However, if you feel faint or lightheaded frequently, a physician might check your blood iron status.
5. Sometimes I get clumps or clots in my blood—is that bad?
Small clots can be normal (dime-sized or smaller). Blood gets old and you're shedding pieces of tissue. If you constantly pass large clots, however, or find yourself soaking pads in minutes, see a doctor as this can mean excessive menstrual bleeding.
6. Am I able to swim or participate in sports on my period?
Yes. You shouldn't allow your period to hold you back from anything. Swimming is made easier with tampons or menstrual cups, and it's no issue to use a pad for other things. Even cramping can be relieved by exercise.
7. Why am I sometimes left with an odour when I am on my period?
Some smell is okay, it's just blood and normal bacteria. But if they smell really strong, it could be a sign of an illness or if your pad has not been changed often enough. Consult a doctor if the smell is pungent or fishy, and attempt to switch the pads on a regular basis.
8. Is it okay to be embarrassed to have my period?
Totally normal, but you don't have to be. Every girl gets their period, and it's a normal and healthy part of life. Talking about it makes the stigma smaller.
9. What do I do if I don't have any pads or tampons—what can I use?
They can be used with soft, clean towels folded thick enough to soak up flow, like cotton cloth or worn-out T-shirts. Keep them clean in a clean bag after washing them with soap and water and, if possible at all, drying out in the sun. This is referred to as the use of reused cloth pads, and done the right way, it's safe.
10. How do I stay clean if I cannot shower every day?
Gently wash your private area from the front to the back with a clean, wet cloth saturated with water once or twice a day. Wash only the outside. Wearing underwear made of a breathable material is also useful.
11. How do I keep the same cloth or pad on all day?
Change it every 6 to 8 hours if at all possible. To minimize irritation, fold over a fresh layer of cloth over the soiled one if you can't. Wash or rinse as soon as you can. Having one pad on for extended periods can lead to infection or rash.
12. What do I do if I lack products or am too shy to ask?
Free period products are available at some schools, community centers, shelters, and even food pantries; these are usually low-key and not judged. You may be able to receive assistance from a teacher, school nurse, or other adult if you are comfortable with them. Many others want to help.
13. Why do my cramps worsen some months?
Cramps happen when your uterus muscles contract to shed its lining. Stress or hormone changes can make them worse. Exercise, a heat pack, and hydration can help. If cramps force you to vomit or miss school regularly, see a doctor.
14. Why do I get bloated or puffy before my period?
Hormonal changes cause temporary water retention. Drinking water and reducing salty foods may help.
15. What do I do if my period bleeds through my clothes at school?
It happens to everyone. If you can, wrap a sweatshirt around your waist and go to the restroom to change your pad or add toilet paper temporarily. If possible, bring an extra pair of underwear.
16. Is it wrong that I use toilet paper instead of a pad for a while?
It's okay only as a very short-term solution. It won't absorb well and falls apart. Change it often and switch to a pad or clean cloth as quickly as possible.
17. What if I don't have a location where I can change my pad or cloth in private?
Try to find brief privacy windows—public restrooms, the school nurse's office, or even asking a friend to stand guard. A few minutes are enough to change safely.
18. How do I avoid rashes when changing isn't an option?
If you can't change often, a thin layer of petroleum jelly (if available) can reduce friction. Loose clothing helps air circulation.
19. What if I can't wash my hands before or after changing?
If you can't access soap and water, use hand sanitizer before and after. If you have neither, try to touch only the clean side of pads or cloths and wash as soon as you can.
20. How do I keep my reusable cloths clean if I can't dry them in the sun?
Sunlight helps kill bacteria, but if it's not available, hang them in the cleanest, driest, most ventilated area you can. Make sure they're fully dry before reuse to prevent infections.
